What is this project?

A simple economic explanation of the industrial waste problem.

🏭 The basic problem

Imagine an industrial factory producing goods. During production, the factory also produces waste.


The factory has to decide what to do with that waste: it can recycle or treat it, which may cost money, or dispose of it.

📈 Where economics comes in

The factory looks at its own costs when making this decision. But waste and pollution can also create costs for people, communities and the environment.


Economics helps us understand these costs and how government policies can change the incentives facing the factory.

🏛️

How can the Government influence this?

Government can change the economic incentives faced by producers.

🧾

Pollution Tax

The government can impose a tax on pollution or waste disposal. This increases the cost of polluting and can encourage factories to reduce waste or choose cleaner alternatives.

Example: A factory pays ₹200 per tonne of taxable waste disposal.
♻️

Recycling Subsidy

The government can provide financial support for recycling or cleaner production. This lowers the effective cost of environmentally friendly activities.

Example: A factory receives ₹100 per tonne of qualifying recycled material.
👥

Why does Social Cost matter to the public?

Pollution can create costs for people who are not involved in the factory's decision.

🏭 Factory decision → 🌫️ Pollution → 👥 Public impact

The factory may only pay the direct cost of disposing of its waste. However, pollution can create additional costs for society. These are called external costs.

🌫️

Health

Pollution can increase health-related costs for people living near affected areas.

💧

Water & Soil

Waste can damage water sources, soil quality and other resources used by communities.

🧹

Public Cleanup

Governments may have to spend public money managing pollution and cleaning affected areas.

🌱

Environment

Pollution can cause environmental damage that does not appear on the factory's own balance sheet.

💡 Why is this useful?

Social cost helps governments understand the broader economic impact of pollution. If a factory considers only its private cost, it may have less incentive to reduce waste.


By considering the costs imposed on the public, policymakers can design measures such as pollution taxes, recycling subsidies and environmental regulations.

Social Cost = Private Cost + External Cost on Society
Our example: The factory's disposal cost is ₹100/tonne. The estimated external cost to society is ₹250/tonne.

Therefore:
₹100 + ₹250 = ₹350 social cost per tonne

The ₹250 is not a direct bill paid by each citizen. It is an illustrative economic estimate of the harm and costs that pollution can impose on society.

♻️ From Waste to Wealth

Waste is not only an environmental issue — it is also an economic issue. Factories respond to costs and incentives. Governments can influence those incentives through policies such as pollution taxes and recycling subsidies.


When the economic costs imposed on society are considered, businesses have a stronger reason to reduce waste, recycle materials and adopt cleaner production methods.


The ultimate idea is simple: instead of seeing waste only as something to throw away, we can look for ways to turn it into an economic resource.
